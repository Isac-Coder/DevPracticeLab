## 2. 🧠 Gramática principal: Lenguaje de debate y matización

### Estructuras para presentar argumentos

| Función | Expresión | Ejemplo |
|---|---|---|
| Presentar posición | I would argue that... | I would argue that access to education is a human right. |
| Posición matizada | It could be said that... | It could be said that both sides have merit. |
| Evidencia | There is evidence to suggest that... | There is evidence to suggest that income inequality is rising. |
| Conceder el punto | Granted, / Admittedly, | Granted, the policy has flaws. However, the alternative is worse. |
| Contraargumentar | While it may be true that... | While it may be true that costs are high, the long-term benefits outweigh them. |
| Consecuencia lógica | It follows that... | If access is unequal, it follows that outcomes will be unequal. |

---

### Distinguir hechos de opiniones

| Tipo | Estructura | Ejemplo |
|---|---|---|
| Hecho verificable | Research shows that... | Research shows that remote work increases productivity in some sectors. |
| Opinión propia | In my view... / I believe... | In my view, the benefits outweigh the risks. |
| Opinión ajena | Some argue that... | Some argue that privacy is less important than security. |
| Posición matizada | It is widely believed that... | It is widely believed that AI will disrupt employment. |
| Contraste | Others maintain that... | Others maintain that automation creates more jobs than it eliminates. |

| Collocation | Traducción | Ejemplo |
|---|---|---|

## 5. 🗣️ Casos de uso reales

### Caso 1 — Debate estructurado: ¿Debería regularse la IA?

> **Situación:** Debate formal en RIWI sobre regulación de inteligencia artificial.

```
Instructor: The motion is: "Artificial intelligence should be regulated by 
             governments." Ana and Carlos argue in favor. Diego and Valentina oppose.
             Ana, open.

Ana:        I would argue that the current absence of AI regulation represents 
             a serious and growing risk. We have evidence to suggest that 
             large language models can be used to generate disinformation 
             at scale, automate cyberattacks, and displace workers without 
             any social safety net in place. It follows that without clear rules, 
             the technology will be developed according to what is profitable, 
             not what is safe or fair.

Diego:      While it may be true that AI carries risks, I would challenge 
             the assumption that government regulation is the appropriate solution.
             Broadly speaking, regulatory bodies move too slowly to keep pace 
             with technological change. Heavy regulation could stifle innovation 
             and push development to jurisdictions with fewer protections — 
             achieving the opposite of what is intended.

Carlos:     Granted, regulation has limitations. However, the alternative — 
             self-regulation by companies whose interests are commercial — 
             is not credible. There is evidence that social media platforms, 
             when left to regulate themselves, prioritized engagement 
             over user wellbeing. To some extent, AI companies face 
             the same structural incentive problem.

Valentina:  Others maintain that industry-led standards, developed 
             collaboratively with governments, are more effective than 
             top-down legislation. In many cases, the technology is 
             already so specialized that lawmakers lack the expertise 
             to regulate it meaningfully. It is debatable whether 
             a government that does not understand a technology 
             can effectively regulate it.

Instructor: Strong arguments on both sides. 
             The nuance here is not whether to regulate, 
             but how and by whom.
             Both sides seem to agree that unchecked development is risky —
             that's your common ground. Argue from there.
```

**Nota gramatical:** `I would argue that`, `there is evidence to suggest`, `it follows that`, `while it may be true that`, `I would challenge the assumption`, `granted... however`, `to some extent`, `others maintain that`, `it is debatable whether` — todas las estructuras de debate distribuidas naturalmente.

### Caso 2 — Artículo de opinión estructurado

> **Situación:** Sofía escribe un artículo de opinión sobre brecha salarial de género.

```
The Gender Pay Gap: Why "Equal Pay for Equal Work" Misses the Point

It is widely acknowledged that women earn less than men on average.
The debate, however, is not about whether this gap exists —
the evidence is clear that it does — but about what it means and what to do about it.

Some argue that the raw pay gap is misleading because it compares 
different roles, industries, and hours worked. 
When these variables are controlled for, they claim, the gap narrows significantly.
There is some truth to this. Admittedly, comparing average earnings 
without controlling for profession or hours is a blunt instrument.

However, I would argue that this framing misses a deeper problem.
The reason women are concentrated in lower-paying sectors 
is not random — it reflects historical patterns of exclusion, 
social expectations around caregiving, and the fact that fields 
dominated by women tend to be undervalued, often immediately 
after women enter them in large numbers (a documented pattern 
sometimes called "devaluation by association").

In my view, achieving genuine equality requires more than equal pay 
for the same role — it requires examining why the same work 
is not valued equally across different contexts.

Others maintain that individual choice explains much of the gap.
I acknowledge this is partly true. But choice operates within constraints.
When caregiving responsibilities fall disproportionately on women,
and when flexible work is penalized with lower pay and slower promotion,
the "choice" framing understates the structural pressures involved.

The evidence is mixed on whether any single policy fully closes the gap.
Parental leave reform, pay transparency legislation, 
and childcare availability have all shown results in various countries.
What seems clear is that the gap will not close on its own.
Not everyone would agree that legislative intervention is necessary —
but the data consistently shows that voluntary corporate action alone 
has not been sufficient.
```

### Caso 3 — Conversación sobre libertad de expresión y límites

> **Situación:** Diego y Ana tienen una conversación honesta sobre dónde están los límites del discurso.

```
Diego:   Do you think there should be limits on freedom of speech?

Ana:     In principle, no. In practice, yes — and I think those aren't contradictory.

Diego:   How so?

Ana:     Freedom of speech protects political dissent, artistic expression, 
          minority viewpoints. Those protections are essential. 
          But it could be said that speech that targets specific people 
          with the intent to harm — like coordinated harassment or 
          incitement to violence — isn't really political speech 
          in the classical sense. It's weaponization.

Diego:   Some would argue that's a slippery slope. 
          Who decides what counts as incitement?

Ana:     That's a valid concern. Broadly speaking, that's why this 
          is best left to democratic legal systems rather than platforms.
          The issue with social media companies making these calls 
          is accountability — they're not democratically elected 
          and their incentives are commercial.

Diego:   I take your point. Where I'd challenge this is:
          democratic systems in some countries have used 
          "incitement" laws to silence legitimate political opposition.
          The tool is only as trustworthy as the institution using it.

Ana:     That's a fair counterpoint. I think we actually agree 
          on the goal — we just disagree on whether existing institutions 
          are trustworthy enough to be given that power.

Diego:   Which means the real debate is about institutional trust, 
          not about freedom of speech itself.

Ana:     Exactly. And that's a much more interesting conversation.
```

### Caso 4 — Guía de debate para estudiantes

> **Situación:** El instructor de RIWI entrega una guía para el módulo de debate en inglés.

```
HOW TO DEBATE RESPECTFULLY AND EFFECTIVELY

A debate is not an argument — it is a structured exchange 
designed to examine an issue from multiple angles. 
Here is a framework for doing it well.

1. SEPARATE THE PERSON FROM THE POSITION
Attack the argument, not the person.
"That reasoning has a flaw" is debate. 
"You're wrong because you don't understand this" is not.

2. ACKNOWLEDGE STRONG POINTS
Saying "that's a strong point, and I'd respond by..." 
is not weakness — it's intellectual honesty.
It also tends to make the audience take you more seriously.

3. DISTINGUISH FACTS FROM OPINIONS
"Studies show..." and "I believe..." are different statements.
Mixing them is a common debate mistake.
Use language that signals which you're doing.

4. USE SUAVIZADORES
Blanket statements invite blanket refusals.
"In many cases, the policy has improved outcomes" 
is easier to defend than "this policy works."
Nuance is not hedging — it's accuracy.

5. FIND AND NAME COMMON GROUND
Most debates have at least one point of agreement.
Finding it narrows the actual disagreement 
and makes the rest of the debate more productive.

6. CONCEDE BEFORE YOU COUNTER
"Granted, that data exists. However, when we look at 
the full context..." is more effective than starting with "But."
It shows you've actually engaged with the other side.

The goal of a good debate is not to win. 
It is to come closer to a clearer understanding of a complex issue.
You don't have to change your view. But you should be changed 
by the process.
```

| Registro | Expresión |
|---|---|
| Muy formal | It could be posited that... / One might contend... |
| Formal | I would argue that... / There is evidence to suggest... |
| Semi-formal | In my view... / Some argue that... |
| Conversacional | I think... / Don't you think...? / What about...? |
| Concesión | Granted... / Admittedly... / Fair enough... |

## 7. ⚠️ Errores frecuentes

### Mezclar `since` (causal) y `since` (temporal)

- `Since the policy was introduced, outcomes have improved.` → temporal
- `Since the policy benefits everyone, it should be adopted.` → causal

Ambas son correctas. Pero en debate formal, `since` causal puede sonar débil — `because` es más directo.

### `Some people think` como argumento de paja

En debate, citar "some people" sin evidencia es una falacia. Sé específico:
- ❌ "Some people say AI is dangerous."
- ✅ "A 2023 report by MIT found that..." / "Critics like [X] argue..."

### `Always` y `never` en argumentos

Evítalos — siempre hay excepciones y debilitan tu argumento.
- ❌ "Regulation always stifles innovation."
- ✅ "Heavy-handed regulation **has, in some cases**, slowed adoption."
