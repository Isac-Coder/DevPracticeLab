---

## 2. 🧠 Gramática principal: Futuro en el pasado (Future in the Past)

### ¿Qué es el futuro en el pasado?

Expresa algo que, desde una perspectiva pasada, era considerado futuro — pero que ya no ocurrirá o cambió.

```
I was going to apply for that job, but I changed my mind.
She had planned to study medicine, but she chose engineering.
He was supposed to present, but he got sick.
```

---

### Estructuras del futuro en el pasado

| Estructura | Uso | Ejemplo |
|---|---|---|
| **was/were going to** | plan que se tenía y cambió | I **was going to** study abroad. |
| **was/were supposed to** | obligación o expectativa no cumplida | She **was supposed to** graduate last year. |
| **had planned to** | plan más formal que cambió | We **had planned to** launch in June. |
| **had intended to** | intención que no se concretó | He **had intended to** become a lawyer. |
| **was/were about to** | estaba a punto de (cuando algo lo interrumpió) | I **was about to** submit when the system crashed. |
| **would** (futuro narrado) | en narración: lo que iba a pasar | She didn't know she **would** become a developer. |

---

### Condicional mixto: pasado irreal → consecuencia presente

```
If I had studied CS earlier, I would be much further along now.
If she hadn't changed careers, she might still be unhappy today.
```

> Esta es la combinación de 3rd y 2nd condicional — la consecuencia está en el presente.

## 5. 🗣️ Casos de uso reales

### Caso 1 — Entrevista sobre trayectoria profesional

> **Situación:** Una mentora de tech entrevista a Valentina para un podcast sobre carreras en tecnología.

```
Host:      So, Valentina — how did you end up in frontend development?

Valentina: It's a funny story. I was going to study graphic design.
            I had applied to two universities and everything.
            But a few months before starting, I attended a coding workshop
            almost by accident — a friend had planned to go and cancelled,
            so I went instead.

Host:      And that changed everything?

Valentina: Completely. Within two hours I was hooked. I had never thought 
            about writing code before. I was supposed to start design school 
            in February, but I deferred my enrollment and applied to RIWI instead.

Host:      That's a significant pivot. Were you afraid?

Valentina: Terrified. I was about to start a path I knew, 
            in a field I liked. I changed to something I barely understood 
            and had no experience in. In retrospect, it was the best decision 
            I've made. But at the time it felt reckless.

Host:      Any regrets looking back?

Valentina: One. I wish I had started coding earlier — even just as a hobby.
            If I had known how much I would enjoy it, 
            I would have started at 15 instead of 22.
            But honestly, if I hadn't taken that path first,
            I might not appreciate this one as much. 
            Things work out in strange ways.

Host:      What advice would you give someone in a similar position — 
            thinking about pivoting?

Valentina: I'd say: the plans you had before were not wasted. 
            Every path teaches you something.
            But if something is pulling you — don't wait until 
            you're 100% sure. You never will be.
```

**Nota gramatical:** `I was going to study`, `had planned to go`, `was supposed to start`, `was about to start` — futuro en el pasado en variantes. `If I had known... I would have started` — 3rd condicional. `If I hadn't taken that path... I might not appreciate` — condicional mixto.

### Caso 2 — Reflexión personal en LinkedIn

> **Situación:** Diego publica una reflexión sobre su cambio de carrera.

```
Three years ago, I was going to be a civil engineer.

I had completed two years of the degree. 
I was supposed to graduate in 2026. 
My family was proud. I had a clear path.

Then I discovered programming. Not through a formal course — 
through boredom during the pandemic. I had intended to just learn 
the basics. I ended up spending six months building things.

I applied to RIWI on a impulse. I was about to talk myself out of it 
when a friend said: "you're already unhappy where you are. 
What do you have to lose?"

I changed paths.

Looking back with the benefit of hindsight, I understand a few things:

1. The two years of engineering weren't wasted. 
   I think differently about systems, structures, and problem-solving 
   because of them. If I hadn't studied engineering, 
   I'd probably be a worse developer today.

2. I wish I had trusted my instincts sooner. 
   I knew something wasn't right. I stayed anyway.
   It turns out that staying wasn't courage — it was just fear of change.

3. The plans I had weren't wrong. They were incomplete.
   I was going to build bridges. Now I build interfaces.
   Both are about connecting things. I'm just faster with the new ones.

If you're sitting on a decision you've been putting off — 
this is the nudge. The path doesn't have to be straight.
It just has to be yours.
```

### Caso 3 — Conversación sobre planes cambiantes

> **Situación:** Carlos y Ana hablan durante el descanso del último módulo de RIWI.

```
Carlos:   Can you believe we're almost done? What are your plans after this?

Ana:      Honestly, they've changed about three times this month.
           I was going to go straight into job applications.
           But then I started a personal project that I'm actually 
           excited about, so now I'm considering freelancing first.

Carlos:   What kind of project?

Ana:      A platform for independent artisans — it connects buyers 
           with local makers. My cousin had planned to build something 
           like this but never did. I figured I could.

Carlos:   That's actually a solid idea. What about you?

Ana:      What are YOUR plans? I've been meaning to ask.

Carlos:   I had intended to apply to a big tech company — 
           one of the multinationals. But after talking to some 
           senior developers, I'm reconsidering. They said the growth 
           at startups tends to be faster in the first few years.
           I was supposed to start applying last week, 
           but I'm still making up my mind.

Ana:      I wish I had done more research on the market before the program.
           I came in thinking I wanted to work in a startup and now 
           I'm not so sure. Everything sounds good when you don't 
           know anything yet.

Carlos:   Ha. If I had known what I know now, I would have asked 
           very different questions at the beginning.

Ana:      Exactly. In retrospect, that's always how it works.
```

### Caso 4 — Artículo: Cómo cambiar de carrera sin perder lo que construiste

> **Situación:** RIWI Blog publica una guía para quienes hacen transición a tech.

```
Career Pivots: What No One Tells You

Most career change articles focus on the destination. 
This one is about what you leave behind — and why that's okay.

MYTH 1: "Your old career was a waste."
It wasn't. Every profession develops transferable skills.
If you were a teacher, you know how to communicate complex ideas clearly.
If you were an accountant, you already think in structured systems.
If you had planned to be a lawyer but ended up in product management,
your ability to argue a position and anticipate objections is an asset.

MYTH 2: "You should have known sooner."
People who say "I knew from age 12 I was going to be a developer" 
are describing their outcome, not predicting everyone's path.
Most people who are now thriving in tech were supposed to be something else.
They were about to give up more times than they'll admit.

MYTH 3: "Starting over means starting from zero."
It doesn't. It means starting from experience, just in a new direction.
If I had started over from zero at 25, with nothing behind me,
I wouldn't have the perspective I have now. The years weren't wasted.
They were invested — in the person who eventually made the pivot.

In retrospect, the question isn't: 
"What if I had started earlier?"
The better question is: "What am I going to do with where I am now?"

Start from there.
```

## 7. ⚠️ Errores frecuentes

### `Was going to` sin el cambio implícito

`I was going to do X` implica que no lo hiciste (o algo lo cambió). Si sí lo hiciste, usa Past Simple:
- *I was going to apply.* → no apliqué
- *I applied.* → lo hice

### `I wish I had` + pasado simple (error)

❌ I wish I started earlier.
✅ I wish I **had** started earlier. (wish + Past Perfect para arrepentimientos pasados)

### Condicional mixto — mezclar mal los tiempos

❌ If I had studied more, I would have a better job now. ✅ (correcto — mixed)
❌ If I have studied more, I would have a better job now. ✗ (incorrecto)
