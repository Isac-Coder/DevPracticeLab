### Repaso integrado de estructuras de C1

**Cleft sentences para énfasis:**
```
It is precisely this interdependence that makes unilateral solutions impossible.
What the pandemic revealed was how fragile global supply chains had become.
```

**Inversión para fuerza retórica:**
```
Never before has the world been so connected — or so vulnerable.
Not only does globalization create wealth, but it also concentrates it.
Rarely have economic and ecological crises aligned so precisely.
```

**Especulación y hipótesis:**
```
Were global supply chains to fragment permanently, the cost would be immense.
Had international cooperation been stronger, the pandemic response would have been faster.
Climate change appears to be accelerating more rapidly than models predicted.
```

**Matización y complejidad:**
```
The extent to which free trade benefits all participants is contested.
In so far as globalization reduces poverty, its record is mixed.
The very mechanisms that enable global coordination can also enable global dysfunction.
```

**Concesión avanzada:**
```
Admittedly, globalization has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty.
For all its benefits, the distributional effects have been deeply unequal.
Granted, no alternative system has demonstrated superior outcomes.
```

**Subjuntivo y exigencia:**
```
It is essential that international institutions be reformed.
The situation demands that all parties negotiate in good faith.
```

## 5. 🗣️ Casos de uso reales

### Caso 1 — Ensayo sobre la paradoja de la globalización

> **Situación:** Valentina escribe el ensayo final del programa sobre globalización.

```
The Paradox of Interconnection: Why the World's Greatest Strength 
Is Also Its Greatest Vulnerability

Never before has the world been so connected.
The shirt you're wearing was probably designed in one country, 
made with materials from three others, assembled in a fourth, 
and shipped to you from a warehouse in a fifth.
The chip in your phone contains minerals mined in the Congo, 
processed in China, designed in California, and assembled in Taiwan.
The food on your table has traveled, on average, 
further than most humans traveled in their entire lives a century ago.

This integration has produced extraordinary outcomes.
Admittedly, globalization has contributed to lifting hundreds of millions 
of people out of extreme poverty over the past four decades — 
particularly in East and Southeast Asia.
The expansion of global trade has made goods cheaper and more accessible.
It has connected researchers, entrepreneurs, and artists 
across distances that once made collaboration impossible.

And yet. It is precisely this interconnection that created 
the conditions for the COVID-19 pandemic to become a global catastrophe 
rather than a regional one.
It was the same supply chains that deliver cheap electronics 
that also collapsed when a single factory region was disrupted.
Not only did globalization distribute wealth — 
it also distributed risk, and not always to those best positioned to absorb it.

The very mechanisms that enable global coordination can also enable global fragility.
A financial system designed to distribute capital efficiently 
is also, in a crisis, a system that distributes contagion efficiently.
An agricultural system optimized for cost and scale 
is simultaneously optimized for systemic vulnerability.

Were the degree of interdependence we have achieved to be unwound,
the cost would be immense — in prosperity, in knowledge sharing, 
in the collaborative capacity that global challenges like climate change require.
But for all its benefits, the distributional effects have been deeply unequal.
The gains from globalization have not been evenly distributed — 
within countries, workers in tradeable sectors have often been left behind 
while capital has been mobile and returns have concentrated.

The question is not whether globalization should continue.
It is whether the institutions governing it can be reformed 
to distribute its benefits more broadly 
and its risks more fairly.

It is essential that this question be taken seriously.
The alternative — a fragmented world of competing blocs, 
each optimizing for its own security at the cost of shared capacity — 
appears likely to be worse for nearly everyone, 
particularly those least able to absorb the transition cost.

What this interconnected world requires is not less connection 
but better governance of the connections we already have.
That is a political problem, not a technical one.
And political problems are solved — when they are solved — 
through sustained, difficult, unglamorous cooperation.
The history of international institutions is not inspiring.
It is also the only record we have of problems of this scale being addressed at all.
```

**Nota gramatical:** Todo el rango de C1 integrado — inversión (`Never before has`, `Not only did`), cleft (`It is precisely this interconnection that`), concesión avanzada (`Admittedly`, `for all its benefits`), especulación (`Were the degree... to be unwound`), subjuntivo (`It is essential that this question be taken`), matización (`The very mechanisms that`), y coherencia argumentativa sostenida.

### Caso 2 — Debate final: ¿Ha fallado la globalización?

> **Situación:** Debate de cierre del programa de inglés avanzado en RIWI.

```
Instructor: Our final debate question: Has globalization failed?
             Ana and Carlos argue it has. Diego and Valentina argue it hasn't.

Ana:        I'll argue that globalization, as practiced, 
             has failed the majority of the world's population.
             Not because integration is inherently harmful — 
             but because the rules governing it were designed by 
             and for the interests of capital, not of labor or of communities.
             
             The record is clear: over the last forty years, 
             global trade has grown dramatically.
             Global inequality between countries has fallen.
             But inequality within countries — including those that 
             "won" by conventional metrics — has risen sharply.
             
             When we say globalization lifted people out of poverty, 
             we are mostly describing the urbanization of rural China.
             That is real and significant.
             It is not the same as saying the system worked fairly.

Carlos:     I'd add: the environmental cost has been externalized.
             Never before has the atmosphere absorbed this much CO2.
             The model of growth-through-trade that globalization enabled 
             was not priced to include ecological limits.
             We have been running a balance sheet that omitted 
             its largest liability.

Valentina:  For all the real criticisms, I'd argue that the question 
             "has it failed?" assumes a credible alternative.
             Admittedly, globalization has produced inequality.
             But what is the comparison case?
             The historical periods of lower global integration — 
             the 1930s, the 1940s — were not notably better 
             for ordinary people.
             
             What seems clear is that the problem is the governance, 
             not the integration itself.
             Had the WTO's rules included enforceable labor standards 
             from the beginning, had the IMF conditionality been less harsh,
             had the gains from trade been redistributed through stronger 
             domestic policy — the outcomes would have been different.

Diego:      I'd agree with Valentina's framing.
             Not only has globalization failed in some respects —
             but it has also succeeded in ways that are now taken for granted.
             The counterfactual matters.
             
             The question worth asking is not "has it failed?" 
             but "failed compared to what, and what would we need to change 
             to make it work better?"
             That's a more actionable question than a verdict.

Instructor: What I hear across both sides is a shared premise:
             the problem is governance, not integration per se.
             That's a more sophisticated position than the headline debate suggests.
             Good work, all.
```

### Caso 3 — Análisis del rol de Colombia en el mundo globalizado

> **Situación:** Carlos escribe sobre la posición de Colombia en la economía global.

```
Colombia in the Global Economy: Asset, Periphery, or Both?

Colombia occupies a paradoxical position in the global economy.
It is simultaneously a country with extraordinary natural assets — 
one of the most biodiverse nations on Earth, 
significant oil and gas reserves, 
a growing tech sector, and a young labor force — 
and a country that has not fully converted those assets 
into broad-based human development.

The commodity dependence that characterizes many Latin American economies 
is visible here. When global oil prices fall, 
the fiscal space for social spending contracts.
The currency appreciation that accompanies commodity booms 
makes manufactured exports less competitive — 
a dynamic economists call "Dutch disease."

The digital economy offers a partial escape from this trap.
Software, design, and services can be exported without ships or pipelines.
The growth of Colombia's tech sector — 
particularly in Medellín and Bogotá — 
is on course to represent a meaningful share of service exports 
within a decade, provided the educational infrastructure 
continues to develop.

This is where programs like RIWI become relevant 
at a macroeconomic level, not just an individual one.
The extent to which a middle-income country can shift 
from commodity dependence to knowledge economy 
depends on whether it can develop human capital quickly enough 
to compete in a global market for talent and services.

Were Colombia to develop the depth of technical talent 
that countries like India and Poland have built, 
the implications for per capita income and social mobility would be significant.
The path is clear enough. What is required is sustained investment 
and sustained political will — 
both of which have historically been inconsistent.

That is a challenge. It is also an opportunity of a specific kind:
one that rewards preparation, persistence, and quality of work.
For anyone in this room, this is worth knowing.
```

### Caso 4 — Carta abierta al mundo — texto de cierre del programa

> **Situación:** Ana escribe una carta abierta como reflexión final del programa.

```
To the World We Are Entering

We are finishing a training program. We are also finishing a year 
in which the world outside this classroom has reminded us repeatedly 
that the problems we will face are not simple ones.

Climate change is not slowing on its own.
Global inequality has not resolved itself.
The technology we are learning to build is already reshaping labor markets 
in ways that will affect the communities we come from.

None of us asked for this complexity. It came with the ticket.

What I want to say to whoever is reading this — 
which may, if you're being honest, only be me —
is that the size of the problems is not a reason for paralysis.
It is a reason for specificity.

You cannot fix climate change. 
You can do the specific work in front of you well, 
with awareness of what it connects to.
You cannot solve global inequality.
You can make choices about where your skills go 
and who benefits from them.

The interconnected world we are entering is not a comfortable one.
Rarely have people entering a labor market faced 
so many genuine uncertainties simultaneously.
But never before has there also been this much distributed capacity — 
this many people, across this many geographies, 
with this much access to knowledge and tools.

What we do with that — whether we build things that concentrate power 
or distribute it, whether we document what we learn 
or hoard it, whether we see the people using our software 
as users to be retained or as people to be genuinely served — 
those choices, made millions of times across an industry, add up to something.

It is essential that we remember that.
Not every day. But enough days.

Good luck. Work well.
Do something worth doing.
```

## 7. ⚠️ Errores frecuentes de C1 — repaso final

### Inversión sin auxiliar correcto

❌ Never the world has been so connected.
✅ Never **has** the world been so connected.

### Subjuntivo con -s

❌ It is essential that this question **is** addressed.
✅ It is essential that this question **be** addressed.

### Cleft con orden incorrecto

❌ What requires this world is better governance.
✅ What this world **requires** is better governance.

### Concesión sin seguimiento

❌ Admittedly, globalization has benefits. (sin contraargumento)
✅ Admittedly, globalization has benefits. **However**, the distributional effects have been unequal.
