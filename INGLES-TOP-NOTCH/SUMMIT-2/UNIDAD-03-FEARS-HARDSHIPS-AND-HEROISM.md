## 2. 🧠 Gramática principal: Reported speech en C1 — complejidad y variación

### El reported speech en C1 va más allá del backshift

En C1 el estilo indirecto incluye:
1. Backshift cuando es necesario
2. Sin backshift cuando la afirmación sigue siendo verdad
3. Elección de verbos de reporte que transmiten actitud del hablante
4. Reported questions, commands, and exclamations
5. Structures with `as` and `according to`

---

### Cuándo NO hacer backshift

Cuando la afirmación es una verdad permanente o una opinión que el hablante aún sostiene, el backshift es opcional:

```
She said that courage is not the absence of fear. (sigue siendo verdad — sin backshift)
He said the human brain is wired for threat detection. (verdad permanente — sin backshift)
They argued that heroism requires witnesses. (posición de debate — sin backshift)
```

---

### Verbos de reporte que expresan la actitud del hablante

| Verbo | Actitud implicada | Ejemplo |
|---|---|---|
| claim | escepticismo | He **claimed** he had no choice. |
| maintain | posición sostenida bajo presión | She **maintained** that the decision was correct. |
| insist | énfasis / resistencia | He **insisted** that fear was not a factor. |
| concede | admitir algo difícil de admitir | She **conceded** that she had been afraid. |
| acknowledge | reconocimiento de hecho | He **acknowledged** that the risk was real. |
| contend | argumento formal | They **contend** that heroism is culturally constructed. |
| dispute | disagreement activo | She **disputes** the characterization of her actions as heroic. |
| reveal | información previamente oculta | He **revealed** that the decision had taken years. |
| reflect | mirada retrospectiva | She **reflected** that fear had shaped her more than courage. |

---

### Reported questions en C1

```
She asked what had driven him to act.          (wh-question)
He wondered whether courage was innate.         (yes/no → whether)
They questioned how anyone could remain calm.   (how + indirect order)
She wanted to know why he had not hesitated.   (why)
```

> ⚠️ Orden afirmativo siempre en preguntas indirectas:
> ❌ She asked what **was he** thinking.
> ✅ She asked what **he was** thinking.

## 5. 🗣️ Casos de uso reales

### Caso 1 — Perfil de heroísmo ordinario (texto)

> **Situación:** Ana escribe sobre lo que realmente es el heroísmo para la clase de inglés avanzado.

```
The Heroism Nobody Films

We have a cultural template for heroism: the dramatic moment, 
the clear moral stakes, the crowd watching.
The firefighter entering the building. The soldier shielding their unit.
The person who dives into the water without thinking.

These are real. They deserve recognition.
But they are not the only — or even the most common — form of heroism.

The nurse who works a double shift during an outbreak 
and still answers every call button. 
The teacher who stays after class with the student 
no one else has time for.
The person who tells the truth in a meeting 
where telling the truth is professionally dangerous.

These people rarely contend that what they're doing is heroic.
Most, if asked, would dispute the characterization entirely.
They would say they were simply doing what needed doing.

But this is precisely what makes ordinary heroism interesting 
from a psychological standpoint. Research on heroic behavior suggests 
that most people who act courageously in difficult situations 
do not experience a dramatic burst of fearlessness — 
they act despite fear, not in the absence of it.

Philip Zimbardo, who studied heroism extensively, maintained 
that the capacity for heroic action exists in most people.
He argued that what distinguishes those who act from those who don't 
is less a fixed character trait than a situational decision: 
the willingness, in that specific moment, to prioritize 
another person's need over one's own comfort or safety.

What this suggests is both encouraging and demanding:
encouraging, because it means heroism is more accessible 
than our cultural mythology implies;
demanding, because it means we are each more responsible 
for the moments we fail to act than we might prefer to believe.
```

**Nota gramatical:** `Most would dispute the characterization entirely`, `He argued that what distinguishes...is less a fixed trait than a situational decision`, `Zimbardo maintained that the capacity exists`, `What this suggests is both encouraging and demanding` — reported speech y estructuras de C1.

### Caso 2 — Entrevista a una persona que superó una adversidad

> **Situación:** Carlos escribe una entrevista reconstructida para clase sobre resiliencia.

```
"I Learned to Fear and Then I Learned to Work"

The following is a reconstructed account based on a real conversation.

She revealed that the hardest part was not the illness itself.
She conceded that she had expected the diagnosis to feel 
like the central event — and it did, for a while.
But she maintained that what stayed with her longer 
was the period after treatment, when the crisis had passed 
and she was left with the question of what to do next.

She acknowledged that before the illness, 
she had spent years working without questioning why.
She reflected that it had taken something close to a life-ending event 
to make her ask what she actually valued.

When asked whether she would want to undo what had happened,
she paused. Then she said she wouldn't — 
which surprised her as much as it surprised the interviewer.

She explained that post-traumatic growth was a real phenomenon —
that many people who survive extreme hardship report 
that their capacity for gratitude, depth of relationships, 
and clarity of purpose increase as a result.
She insisted that this was not the same as saying the trauma was worth it.
She distinguished carefully: not "I'm glad it happened" 
but "I became someone I couldn't have become otherwise."

She was asked what advice she would give.
She reflected for a moment and then said something worth recording:
"The fear doesn't go away. You just learn to work while it's there."
```

### Caso 3 — Análisis psicológico del miedo (artículo)

> **Situación:** Diego escribe sobre la psicología del miedo para la clase.

```
Fear as Information: A Reframe

The dominant cultural message about fear is: overcome it.
Push through. Be brave. Don't let it stop you.

This is useful advice — as far as it goes.
The problem is that it frames fear as purely obstructive:
something to be defeated, suppressed, or ignored.

Research in clinical psychology suggests something more nuanced.
Fear, in its evolutionary function, is information.
The threat response — the fight-or-flight activation —
exists because it once kept humans alive.
The question is not whether to have it, but what to do with it.

A psychologist specializing in anxiety disorders once contended 
that the most effective approach to fear was not suppression but curiosity.
She maintained that the patients who improved most consistently 
were those who learned to ask "what is this fear telling me?" 
rather than "how do I make this stop?"

This distinction matters practically.
Fear of public speaking, for example, often reveals something useful: 
a genuine desire to communicate well, paired with uncertainty about whether one can.
The information embedded in that fear — "this matters to me" — 
is worth hearing. The physiological response — the racing heart, 
the constricted throat — is not necessarily a signal to stop.
It may simply be the body registering that something important is happening.

The reframe is not from "fear is bad" to "fear is good."
It is from "fear is an obstacle" to "fear is data."
What you do with data is different from what you do with an obstacle.
You analyze it. You ask what it's telling you.
And then you decide what to do next.
```

### Caso 4 — Debate: ¿El heroísmo requiere testigos?

> **Situación:** El equipo de RIWI debate si el heroísmo necesita reconocimiento para ser real.

```
Instructor: Is an act heroic if no one witnesses it?
             Or does heroism require recognition to exist?

Ana:        I'd argue that heroism is defined by the nature of the act, 
             not the presence of an audience.
             The person who finds a wallet and returns it 
             when no one is watching performs an act that is — 
             by any meaningful definition — more honest than the same act 
             performed under observation.
             Heroism without witnesses may actually be purer heroism.

Carlos:     That's philosophically interesting, but it ignores 
             the social function of heroism.
             Heroes serve as models. A heroic act that no one knows about 
             cannot inspire anyone. Part of what heroism does — 
             what it's for, in social terms — is provide examples 
             of what people are capable of.
             Without that function, does the category still make sense?

Valentina:  I'd distinguish between the act and the category.
             The act can be genuinely heroic without anyone knowing.
             Whether we call it heroic depends on whether we know.
             These are different things.

Diego:      Zimbardo contended that heroism is fundamentally social — 
             that it requires opposition (something to stand against)
             and risk (something to lose).
             Whether it requires recognition he was less clear on.
             I suspect the answer is: it doesn't require recognition 
             to be real, but it does require recognition to be useful 
             as a social concept.

Instructor: What I'm hearing is that you agree on the act 
             but disagree on the concept.
             That's actually a philosophically sophisticated distinction.
```

## 6. ⏱️ Reported speech en distintos contextos de C1

| Tipo | Ejemplo |
|---|---|
| Afirmación con verdad permanente | She maintained that courage is acting despite fear. |
| Concesión difícil | He conceded that he had been terrified. |
| Disputa | She disputed the characterization of her actions as heroic. |
| Reflexión | He reflected that fear had shaped him more than he'd realized. |
| Pregunta indirecta | They wondered whether heroism required recognition. |
| Exclamación reportada | She expressed that the experience had been transformative. |

## 7. ⚠️ Errores frecuentes

### Orden en preguntas indirectas

❌ She asked what **was he** afraid of.
✅ She asked what **he was** afraid of.

### Backshift innecesario con verdades permanentes

❌ He said that courage **had been** the opposite of fearlessness.
✅ He said that courage **is** the opposite of fearlessness. (verdad permanente)

### `Contend` vs `content`

- `contend` → verbo: argumentar, mantener una posición
- `content` → adjetivo: satisfecho; o sustantivo: contenido
❌ She contented that the view was wrong.
✅ She **contended** that the view was wrong.
