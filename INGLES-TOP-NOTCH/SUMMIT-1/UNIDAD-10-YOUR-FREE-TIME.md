## 2. 🧠 Gramática principal: Cláusulas nominales (Nominal Clauses)

### ¿Qué son?

Las cláusulas nominales funcionan como sustantivos dentro de la oración — pueden ser sujeto, objeto o complemento. En B2 te permiten construir argumentos más complejos y precisos que con simples sustantivos.

---

### Tipos de cláusulas nominales

**That-clauses (lo que alguien dice, piensa, cree):**
```
Subject:   That leisure has intrinsic value is widely recognized.
Object:    I believe that rest is not a luxury.
Complement: The problem is that we've confused productivity with worth.
```

**Wh-clauses (lo que, cómo, dónde, por qué):**
```
Subject:   What we do in our free time reveals our values.
Object:    I've never understood why we feel guilty for resting.
Complement: The question is whether genuine leisure is possible in a connected world.
```

**Whether/if-clauses (si):**
```
Object:    I'm not sure whether passive rest is as restorative as active leisure.
Subject:   Whether burnout is a medical condition or a cultural phenomenon is debated.
```

---

### Frases nominales abstractas vs. cláusulas nominales

| Frase nominal abstracta | Cláusula nominal equivalente |
|---|---|
| The value of leisure | That leisure has value |
| The question of work-life balance | Whether work and life can be balanced |
| The difficulty of disconnecting | How difficult it is to disconnect |
| The assumption that productivity matters | The assumption that productivity defines worth |

Las frases nominales son más compactas; las cláusulas son más explícitas. En B2, alterna entre ambas.

## 5. 🗣️ Casos de uso reales

### Caso 1 — Ensayo filosófico sobre el derecho al ocio

> **Situación:** Valentina escribe un ensayo para la clase de inglés B2.

```
On the Right to Do Nothing Useful

There is a particular guilt that afflicts high-achievers:
the sense that time not optimized is time wasted.

What troubles me about this guilt is not that it's irrational.
In a competitive economy, the opportunity cost of rest is real.
The problem is that treating leisure as purely instrumental —
as recovery in service of performance — 
eliminates the intrinsic value of experience.

Whether genuine leisure is possible in a world of permanent connectivity 
is a question that I find genuinely difficult to answer.
Physically, I can close the laptop. Mentally, that is considerably harder.
What remains is a kind of pseudo-rest: the body is still, 
the mind is rehearsing tomorrow's meeting.

Csikszentmihalyi's concept of "flow" describes a state 
of absorbed engagement that is intrinsically rewarding — 
not because of what it produces, but because of what it is.
What his research consistently shows is that humans report 
their greatest life satisfaction not during passive rest 
but during active, freely chosen engagement with meaningful challenges.

That leisure has this quality — genuinely restorative, 
genuinely valuable in itself — seems clear.
That our current culture makes it easy to access is less certain.

The case I want to make is not for idleness.
It is for agency: the recognition that what we choose to do 
with unstructured time reveals, more honestly than anything else,
what we actually value.

That productivity culture has made this kind of honesty harder 
is, I think, one of its most underacknowledged costs.
```

**Nota gramatical:** `What troubles me about this guilt`, `Whether genuine leisure is possible`, `What remains is`, `What his research consistently shows is`, `That leisure has this quality`, `That our current culture makes it easy`, `That productivity culture has made this harder` — cláusulas nominales sistemáticas en un ensayo filosófico.

### Caso 2 — Conversación sobre burnout y descanso

> **Situación:** Diego y Ana hablan durante el último sprint antes de vacaciones.

```
Diego:    I can't remember the last time I did something 
           with no purpose other than enjoying it.

Ana:      That's the burnout sign nobody talks about —
           it's not just exhaustion. 
           It's the inability to experience things as enjoyable anymore.

Diego:    That's exactly it. Whether what I feel is exhaustion 
           or something more structural, I don't know.
           But I notice that I can't switch off. Even on weekends,
           what I'm thinking about is what I haven't finished.

Ana:      I read somewhere that the mind needs genuinely unscheduled time —
           not rest planned around productivity, 
           but time with no agenda whatsoever.
           What happens in that space is where creativity comes from.

Diego:    How do you actually do that without feeling guilty?

Ana:      Honestly? I still feel the guilt. 
           What I've learned to do is sit with it rather than react to it.
           The guilt is a signal from the culture, not from reality.
           That rest is productive in its own way is something 
           I have to remind myself of regularly.

Diego:    What do you actually do — that counts as real leisure for you?

Ana:      Swimming. And cooking elaborate things I have no excuse to make.
           The point is there's no deliverable at the end.
           I can't show anyone the swim or the soup.
           That's exactly what makes it rest.

Diego:    I've been meaning to learn something just for the sake of learning it.
           Not for my portfolio. Not to put on LinkedIn.
           Just... because.

Ana:      Do that. Whether it makes you better at your job is beside the point.
           The point is that you chose it freely.
```

### Caso 3 — Artículo sobre la cultura del hustle

> **Situación:** Carlos escribe un análisis de la cultura del trabajo sin descanso.

```
Hustle Culture and Its Discontents

What we celebrate in hustle culture is, at its core, 
the substitution of identity for achievement.
That working constantly is admirable is an assumption 
so embedded in certain professional communities 
that questioning it feels like advocating for laziness.

It isn't.

Whether genuine high performance is compatible with 
chronic overwork is an empirical question — and the evidence 
consistently suggests it isn't. Research on cognitive performance 
indicates that sustained working hours above roughly 50 per week 
produce output comparable to that of 55 hours but with 
significantly higher error rates. 

What hustle culture systematically ignores is the distinction 
between being busy and being effective.
That these are different things is not controversial in productivity research.
It is, however, apparently controversial in LinkedIn culture.

The emotional logic of hustle culture is understandable.
Whether success comes from effort is something most people want to believe — 
because it means the outcome is within their control.
If you work hard enough, you will succeed.
The comforting implication is that failure reflects insufficient effort, 
not structural disadvantage.

That this is partially true makes it more dangerous, not less.
The partial truth serves as camouflage for the whole claim —
and the whole claim asks people to sacrifice their health, relationships, 
and capacity for joy on a bet that working more will eventually be enough.

For most people, it won't be. 
And what they will have traded for that discovery 
cannot be recovered.
```

## 6. ⏱️ Cláusulas nominales en distintos tiempos verbales

| Tiempo | Ejemplo |
|---|---|
| Present | That rest is essential is well-established. |
| Past | Whether the policy worked was never evaluated. |
| Future | What will matter is whether you rested enough. |
| Perfect | That burnout rates have risen is now widely documented. |
| Modal | Whether leisure can be truly restorative in a connected world remains debatable. |

---

## 7. ⚠️ Errores frecuentes

### `That`-clause como sujeto — necesita verbo singular

❌ That people work too much are a cultural problem.
✅ That people work too much **is** a cultural problem.

### `Whether` vs `if` en cláusulas nominales formales

- En registro formal y en posición de sujeto, se prefiere `whether`:
  - ✅ Whether leisure has intrinsic value is debated.
  - ❌ If leisure has intrinsic value is debated.
- En posición de objeto, ambas son aceptables:
  - ✅ I don't know whether / if rest is enough.

### Omitir `that` en registro formal

En textos académicos, omitir `that` puede crear ambigüedad:
- ❌ I believe rest is not optional.
- ✅ I believe **that** rest is not optional. (más claro en registro formal)
