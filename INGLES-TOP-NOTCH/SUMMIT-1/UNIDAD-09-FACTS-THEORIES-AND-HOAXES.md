## 2. 🧠 Gramática principal: Modalidad epistémica (grados de certeza)

### ¿Qué es la modalidad epistémica?

Es el sistema lingüístico que expresa qué tan seguro estás de algo. Calibrar tu lenguaje al nivel de certeza que la evidencia justifica es la marca del comunicador intelectualmente honesto — y es esencial al hablar de hechos vs. teorías.

---

### Escala de certeza

| Nivel | Marcador | Ejemplo |
|---|---|---|
| Certeza total | certainly, definitely, clearly, without doubt | The earth is clearly not flat. |
| Muy probable | almost certainly, very likely, evidently | The source almost certainly has a conflict of interest. |
| Probable | probably, likely, in all likelihood | This claim is likely exaggerated. |
| Posible | possibly, may, might, could, perhaps | The image could have been manipulated. |
| Incierto | uncertain, unclear, debatable, contested | The evidence on this is unclear. |
| Improbable | unlikely, doubtful, questionable | This claim is highly questionable. |
| No hay evidencia | no evidence, unsubstantiated, unfounded | The claim is entirely unsubstantiated. |

---

### Estruturas para evaluar afirmaciones

```
There is [strong / limited / no] evidence to suggest that...
The claim [appears to / seems to / is likely to] be [accurate / exaggerated / false].
This source [may / might] have a [conflict of interest / agenda].
It is [worth noting / important to recognize] that...
What cannot be verified is...
The burden of proof for this claim has [not been met / been met by...].
```

## 5. 🗣️ Casos de uso reales

### Caso 1 — Guía de evaluación crítica de información

> **Situación:** Ana y Diego crean una guía para el módulo de comunicación de RIWI.

```
HOW TO EVALUATE A CLAIM BEFORE SHARING IT

LEVEL 1: SOURCE CHECK
Who published this? When? What is their track record?
What do they have to gain from this claim being believed?

A headline confirming what you already believe should trigger more skepticism,
not less. Confirmation bias leads us to accept convenient information 
with less scrutiny than we apply to uncomfortable claims.

LEVEL 2: EVIDENCE HIERARCHY
Not all evidence is equal. A rough hierarchy:
- Peer-reviewed studies with large samples, replicated by independent researchers: strong
- Single studies not yet replicated: promising, not conclusive
- Expert consensus in a relevant field: meaningful, not infallible  
- Individual expert opinion: worth noting, not equivalent to data
- Anecdotal evidence ("I know someone who..."): weak, however emotionally compelling
- No cited source: treat as opinion until verified

LEVEL 3: CORRELATION VS. CAUSATION
This is likely the most frequently misunderstood distinction in public discourse.
Two phenomena occurring together does not mean one causes the other.
Ice cream sales and drowning rates both peak in summer.
Neither causes the other — both are caused by heat.
Any causal claim requires a plausible mechanism and elimination of alternatives.

LEVEL 4: WHAT IS MISSING?
Cherry-picking selects evidence supporting a claim while ignoring contradictory evidence.
A claim supported by some studies but contradicted by the majority 
is not well-supported — even if the supporting studies are real.

LEVEL 5: FALSIFIABILITY
A well-formed claim should be falsifiable — 
there should be evidence that, if it existed, would cause you to revise it.
If no evidence could change your mind, you're not reasoning.
You're defending a belief against inquiry.
```

### Caso 2 — Análisis de una teoría de conspiración popular

> **Situación:** Carlos escribe un análisis de la estructura de las teorías de conspiración.

```
Why Conspiracy Theories Are Hard to Debunk

Conspiracy theories are not simply false beliefs. 
They are epistemically structured in a way that makes them 
extraordinarily resistant to correction.

Consider the standard architecture:
1. A claim is made that powerful actors are concealing the truth.
2. Evidence against the claim is dismissed as fabricated by those same actors.
3. The absence of evidence becomes evidence of how well the conspiracy is hidden.
4. Anyone questioning the theory is framed as either deceived or complicit.

This structure is unfalsifiable by design. 
There is no possible evidence that could disprove it within its own logic —
which means it cannot be engaged with as a normal factual claim.

Research in cognitive psychology suggests several factors 
that may contribute to susceptibility to conspiratorial thinking:
- The need to make sense of chaotic or threatening events.
- Distrust of established institutions (often justified, sometimes generalized).
- A sense that simple explanations are more credible than complex ones.
- The social dimension: shared belief creates community.

What is almost certainly not useful is ridicule.
Evidence suggests that mocking people for their beliefs 
makes them less likely to revise them, not more.

What may be more useful is addressing the underlying need:
the desire for explanatory coherence, the distrust of institutions,
the need to belong to a community with shared knowledge.

This is considerably harder than fact-checking.
But fact-checking alone clearly has not resolved the problem.
```

### Caso 3 — Debate sobre libertad de expresión y desinformación

> **Situación:** Debate en clase sobre si las plataformas deberían eliminar desinformación.

```
Instructor: Should social media platforms be required to remove 
             medical misinformation? And who decides what counts?

Ana:        The case for removal seems straightforward when the misinformation 
             is causing measurable harm — vaccine hesitancy linked to 
             preventable disease outbreaks, for example.
             But "who decides" is the genuine difficulty.
             Platforms are private companies, not democratic institutions.
             Their decisions about what counts as misinformation 
             are not subject to the processes we'd want for something 
             with this level of social impact.

Carlos:     I'd add that what appears to be misinformation sometimes isn't.
             There were claims during the COVID pandemic that were 
             suppressed by platforms as misinformation 
             and later found to be scientifically contested at minimum.
             The epistemic humility required to say 
             "the evidence on this is currently uncertain" 
             is different from "this is false — remove it."

Valentina:  That's true, but it seems to argue for better processes, 
             not for inaction. The alternative — 
             leaving clearly false medical claims to spread freely — 
             has demonstrably cost lives.

Diego:      What I find almost certainly true is that there's no clean solution.
             Every option involves tradeoffs between 
             free expression, accuracy, institutional power, and harm.
             The question is probably not "should this be regulated" 
             but "what regulatory framework would be better 
             than what currently exists."
             And that question is genuinely hard.
```

### Caso 4 — Artículo sobre la posverdad

> **Situación:** Valentina escribe un ensayo sobre la era de la posverdad.

```
Post-Truth: What It Means and Why It Matters

The Oxford Dictionaries' 2016 word of the year was "post-truth."
The term describes a context in which objective facts 
are less influential in shaping public opinion 
than appeals to emotion and personal belief.

It is almost certainly an overstatement to say that facts no longer matter.
What is more likely accurate is that the relationship between 
factual claims and public belief has become more contested.

Several factors probably contribute to this:

First, the volume of information available has increased 
far more rapidly than our capacity to evaluate it.
When a false claim and its debunking both reach millions of people simultaneously,
correction loses the advantage that rarity once gave it.

Second, trust in traditional information authorities 
has declined — often for understandable reasons.
When institutions that claimed authority have misled people,
the resulting skepticism is rational.
The problem is that generalized distrust, applied indiscriminately,
leaves people without a reliable framework for distinguishing 
credible claims from unreliable ones.

Third, social media architecture is likely designed to maximize engagement,
and emotionally activating content — including false content — 
tends to be more engaging than accurate but boring content.
This creates a structural incentive to spread rather than verify.

The solution is probably not primarily technological.
It is almost certainly not primarily individual either.
What is needed is something closer to civic infrastructure:
institutions with genuine independence, accountable processes, 
and consistent standards for evaluating evidence — 
that the public has reason to trust, because they have earned that trust.

Whether we can rebuild that under current conditions is genuinely unclear.
```

## 7. ⚠️ Errores frecuentes

### Afirmar sin calibrar

❌ "The government is hiding the truth."
✅ "There is limited transparent evidence on this, which may indicate institutional opacity — or a lack of public documentation." (calibrado, no conspiratorio)

### Confundir correlación con causalidad

❌ "Countries with more doctors have higher disease rates, so doctors make people sick."
✅ "Countries with more doctors tend to have more reported disease — likely because better healthcare systems identify and track illness more effectively."

### `Fewer` vs `less` con evidencia/datos

- `fewer studies` (contable)
- `less evidence` (incontable)
❌ "There is less studies on this."
✅ "There are **fewer** studies on this." / "There is **less** evidence on this."
